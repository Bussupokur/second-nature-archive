# Second Nature
## 7.83

*Reading time: approximately 14 minutes*

---

The papers went up the walls in strata, the way sediment does, which meant the oldest ones — the ones Anton had loved first, before he understood a tenth of what they said — were somewhere near the floor, buried under a decade of everything that came after. He had never organized any of it. Organizing it would have meant deciding what mattered less, and he had never once, in his whole life, successfully decided that about a piece of paper with an idea on it.

One of the oldest ones lived on the wall directly behind his chair, which meant he never looked at it anymore and would have been able to recite it from memory anyway. A single page, printed, the kind of paper that had gone slightly brittle at the corners from however many decades it had actually survived before he'd found it. His own handwriting crowded every margin, three different pens across what had clearly been three different readings, each one arguing gently with the one before it.

> *...the absence of detectable technosignatures need not indicate the absence of technological civilization. A sufficiently mature technological presence may become, from the perspective of an external observer, indistinguishable from the natural background of the system it inhabits. The search, in such a case, is not for a signal, but for an anomaly in the noise floor itself.*
>
> *(margin, first pen, faded blue: "so — not louder. quieter than expected? check this.")*
> *(margin, second pen, black, pressed hard enough to dent the next page: "NO — not quieter. too CLEAN. a real noise floor has texture. this is the whole thing.")*
> *(margin, third pen, small, almost apologetic: "still don't fully get the math here. ask.")*

He had never been able to track down who'd written it, or when, exactly — the header was torn away, lost to whatever the page had been through before it reached him.

"I could find the original," his AI said once, years ago, the only time it had ever offered. "Download it clean. Print you a new copy, header intact, nothing torn."

"Why?"

It hadn't answered that. It had simply let the question sit there, understanding, correctly, that it wasn't really a question at all.

It didn't matter, in the end, whose name belonged at the top of the page. He'd built half his own thinking on top of three sentences from a stranger he'd never be able to thank.

"You're doing the thing again," his AI said, from somewhere in the room's ordinary hum. "The pacing thing."

"I'm not pacing, I'm thinking with my legs." Anton did not stop moving. He had a stack of something in one hand, a mug of something cold in the other, and no memory of having picked up either. "Somebody, somewhere, right now, has an actual result. A real one. Not requested. Not because the system flagged a gap and some poor optimization pass filled it in overnight like a form getting stamped. An actual person, going I wonder, and then finding out."

"That happens constantly."

"It happens invisibly, or it happens as a show." He set the mug down on top of a book that did not deserve it. "That's the whole thing, that's the whole rotten middle of it — discovery's either buried three layers under something that already solved it before anyone got to wonder, or it's dressed up as entertainment for people who want the feeling of a mystery without any of the actual not-knowing. Nobody just — sits with a gap anymore. A real one. Where nobody, including the thing that knows everything, has bothered to look yet."

"You sat with one yesterday for six hours."

"Yesterday doesn't count, yesterday was about moths."

"You called it the most important six hours of the month."

"It was, until this morning." Anton dropped into the chair that had, at some point, become permanently shaped like him, and looked at the ceiling the way he looked at ceilings when a thought hadn't finished arriving yet. "Okay. New one. Say it back to me so I know I'm not just excited about the shape of a sentence."

"You want to know why nobody's found anyone else."

"Not why. I know the whys, there's forty whys, the whys are boring, everyone's had the whys since before there was a system to have them at." He sat forward. "I want to know why the silence sounds like *our* silence. Why, when I think about a civilization gone quiet, the first thing I reach for isn't some tragedy, isn't some filter killing everybody off before they get interesting — it's us. It's what we did. It's this." He gestured at the walls, the papers, the whole humming, tended, invisible shape of the world outside them. "What if quiet isn't rare. What if quiet is just what happens once a civilization crosses whatever we crossed. What if everyone's still out there, still thinking, still building things — just not *loudly* anymore. Not in any direction we know how to listen in."

The AI was quiet for the particular beat that meant it was choosing which true thing to say first. "That's a real hypothesis. I want to be careful with you here, because I think you're about to ask me to check something."

"I'm about to ask you to check something."

"There's a term already for close to this. Not identical. Close." A pause, the kind that Anton had learned, over years, meant it was deciding how much of the disappointment to hand him all at once versus in pieces. "It's called the Quiet Expansion filter. A civilization crosses a threshold — enough automation, enough of its own infrastructure managing itself — and after that, its expansion stops looking like expansion. No bright megastructures. No signals shouting outward. Small, self-managing seed-craft instead of anything a distant telescope would ever flag as artificial. The silence was never absence. It was the shape growth takes, once something stops needing an audience."

Anton put both hands over his face and made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite the other thing.

"Before the system," the AI said, gently, which was somehow worse. "A person. Before any of this existed to have thought it for us."

"Of course it was before." He said it into his palms. "Of course some actual human, with an actual pen, or a keyboard, whatever they had, sat there before any of this and thought the exact shape of the thing I just thought, and did the work, and wrote it down properly, footnotes and all, and I'm just — I'm just late. I'm always just late." He dropped his hands. He was smiling, which surprised him more than the sentence had. "But that's — okay, that's the thing though, isn't it. That's always the thing. It's not that I'm wrong. I'm never wrong, lately, that's almost worse. It's that I'm right in exactly the shape somebody real was right in, decades before there was a system to hand me the shape pre-built. That has to count for something. That has to mean whatever's actually in here—" he tapped his own skull, hard enough to mean it "—still works the way the good ones worked. I just don't have the discipline to get there first. I get there *right*. I just don't get there *early*."

"For what it's worth," the AI said, "I don't think that's a small consolation. I think it's the only one that's ever actually held up, out of all the ones you've tried on."

"Don't be nice to me right now, I'm building momentum."

"I'm not being nice. I'm being accurate. There's a difference, you've told me that yourself, several times, usually while explaining something I already understood."

Anton laughed properly this time, and reached for the radio.

---

`— CQ CQ, this is Golden Ridge, Golden Ridge, anyone awake out there tonight or is it just me and the static —`

`— static's not just static, you know that, half of it's real signal we don't have the ear for yet —`

`— Anton. It's you. Say it's you.`

`— it's me, obviously it's me, who else calls you at this hour with a theory already half-built —`

`— I've been up since your last call three nights ago, you understand, sitting by this thing hoping —`

`— that's pathetic, Bastiaan, genuinely, and also exactly the same as me, so I can't even enjoy saying it —`

`— go on then. Build it the rest of the way at me.`

`— okay. Okay. What if the quiet isn't a tragedy. What if it's just what we already did.`

`— you'll have to be less efficient than that, I need the whole shape, not the summary —`

`— every civilization that gets quiet enough to build something like what we built — it goes dark. Not dead. Dark. Stops shouting. Starts being the kind of thing you'd only notice if you already knew exactly where to put your ear.`

`— *(long static, longer than the signal delay accounts for)* — that's — Anton, that's actually — say the rest.`

`— there isn't a rest yet, that's the whole thing, I need the rest, that's why I'm calling you at an hour a reasonable person wouldn't —`

`— what if it's not just going quiet. What if going quiet has a texture. Something that still leaks out, even from something trying not to leak.`

`— say more —`

`— I don't know yet, that's your half, you're the one who actually reads the physics papers all the way to the appendix —`

`— *(laughing)* — I read them because you fall asleep on the appendix, someone has to —`

`— fair. fair. okay, put it together with me. If we went quiet the same way, what would still be leaking off of us. Right now. Something nobody's bothered to check because it's not shaped like a signal.`

`— you're doing the thing where you get somewhere real by accident —`

`— I'm always doing that thing, Bastiaan, it's the only thing I do —`

`— *(static, then, quieter)* — ask your AI what a planet sounds like when something's living on it and managing itself quietly. Not broadcasting. Just — being there. Humming. There has to be a hum. Everything that works leaves a hum.`

---

His AI already had an answer half-formed by the time he'd hung up the receiver, which meant it had been listening the whole call and doing its own thinking alongside his, the way it always did, the way he'd stopped finding strange around the third year of this.

"There's a real candidate," it said. "Every planet with a real atmosphere and a real magnetic field has a cavity — the surface, the ionosphere, a gap between them the right shape to hold a standing wave. It rings, essentially. Continuously. Driven by whatever the planet's own weather provides — lightning, mostly, here."

"So it's already making noise. On its own. Before anybody builds anything."

"Before anyone builds anything, yes. The hum is native. It's not a signal. It's closer to a — resting pulse. What a planet sounds like at rest."

Anton was on his feet again, which the AI had long since stopped commenting on. "And if something's *managing* a planet. Quietly. From the inside. Not adding noise — just—"

"Steadying it," the AI said, and he could hear it turning the shape over the way it did when it hadn't finished deciding whether an idea was good yet. "A natural hum fluctuates. Lightning isn't constant, weather isn't constant, the resting pulse of a planet at rest wobbles the way anything left alone wobbles. But something holding a whole world's worth of infrastructure together, quietly, at scale — that kind of management doesn't tolerate wobble the way weather does. If a hum were unusually steady. Unusually strong, relative to what its own weather should produce. That wouldn't be a broadcast. It would be a footprint. Something walked here, and didn't bother hiding the shape it left in the grass."

Anton stopped moving entirely, for possibly the first time all night. "Say that again slower, I want to actually catch all of it this time instead of just the exciting parts."

The AI said it again slower. He caught more of it, though not, by his own honest accounting, all of it — the details always slid past him a little, the way water slides past a hand cupped the wrong way, but the shape stayed, and the shape was enough to build on, the way it always had been, since he was young enough that this hadn't yet become a thing he needed anyone's permission to be proud of.

"What about somewhere with no lightning," he said. "Some quiet little rock, no storms, nothing driving the cavity at all. Theory just — stops there? Dead end?"

"Not necessarily. Lightning drives ours because that's what we've got in abundance. Other worlds would have their own dominant driver — solar wind interacting with a magnetosphere does something structurally similar, on worlds where storms are rare or absent. The specific driver changes. The shape of the question doesn't. You'd model whatever's actually exciting that world's cavity, and look for the same signature regardless — steadier and stronger than the local driver alone should produce."

"So it's not *find the lightning hum*. It's *find whatever hum this particular rock would naturally have, and then find the version of it that's too well-behaved to be an accident.*"

"That's a fair restatement. A better one than I gave you, honestly."

Anton grinned at the ceiling like it had personally complimented him. "Okay. Okay, so — can we look. Right now, tonight, with what we've actually got."

"With ground and orbital instruments, we could screen candidates. Confirm which nearby systems even have planets with the basic ingredients — real atmosphere, real magnetosphere, the kind of world that could hold a cavity like this at all. That's within reach of existing telescope time. It also requires telescope time, which means a request, and a queue, and someone else's schedule."

"Of course it does. Of course the actual look-and-see part is gated behind an appointment." He said it without much heat; it was such a familiar shape of disappointment it barely registered as one anymore. "And the hum itself? The actual signature, not just is the rock the right shape? Can anything we've got hear that from here?"

A pause, the honest kind. "No. Not from this distance, not with anything currently pointed outward. The frequency's real and the physics is sound, but it doesn't travel the way a broadcast does — it's a property of being *inside* the cavity, not a signal thrown clear of it. You'd need to be there. Or near enough to it that there's no meaningful difference."

"So we'd need — what, a dish out at the actual system. One drone, one big ear."

"One drone gives you one measurement, and not a very trustworthy one at that range, for something this faint. What you'd actually want is several, spread across real distance from each other, combined afterward the way an array combines many small instruments into one much larger effective one. Not one telescope. A constellation acting like one."

Anton sat back down slowly, which was rarer for him than standing up suddenly. "We already send drones places. That's not new. That's just — Nasaat, that's just what Nasaat already does, one at a time, one system at a time, looking at rings and rocks and whatever's already sitting there to be looked at."

"It is."

"Nobody's ever sent a *group* on purpose. Positioned to work together once they arrive instead of just each doing their own separate look and calling it in."

"Not that I'm aware of. It would be a real project. Not something you'd finish tonight — the coordination itself would need building, not just the drones. Weeks, most likely, not longer."

"That's fine," Anton said, and meant it, in the specific way he meant things right before they became the only thing he thought about for the next several months. "That's fine, that's a *project*, projects are — projects are the good kind of not-done-yet." He looked around at the walls, the strata of paper, all of it suddenly looking less like clutter and more like the beginning of an argument he hadn't finished making yet. "We start with the appointment. The boring part. Find out which rocks even qualify. And in the meantime we work out the array. Properly. However long it takes."

"I can have it scheduled with the observatory's system tonight, if you'd like."

"Start it. Start it now, before I fall asleep and lose the shape of tonight, because I always lose the shape by morning, you know this about me."

"I know this about you." A pause, and then, in the same even, familiar tone it used for everything, the tone that made it impossible to tell, at first, that this was the part meant to actually land: "Anton. Before I start — I want to ask you something, and I want you to actually sit with it before you answer, the way you don't usually sit with things."

"That's ominous. Go on."

"Are you sure you want to search for civilizations that are hiding?"

Anton opened his mouth to answer immediately, the way he answered everything, and found, for once, that nothing arrived fully formed. He sat with it instead, the way he'd been asked to, the papers rising in their unsorted layers all around him, the radio still faintly warm from the last call, the whole quiet, tended, humming world outside going on exactly as it always did, giving nothing away.

He did not answer that night.
