# Second Nature
## Seven Months

*Reading time: approximately 13 minutes*

---

The shop smelled the way it had always smelled, the way Anselm suspected it would go on smelling long after he stopped being the one keeping the stove lit — spruce shavings, hide glue warmed just past where it wanted to be, and underneath all of it, faint and permanent, the particular must of wood that had been drying in the same rafters since before he was born. He had inherited the smell along with the tools, along with the bench, along with the small, stubborn conviction that an instrument built quickly was an instrument that had already decided not to be honest with whoever eventually played it. The conviction itself was older than any single person who had ever held it. His family traced the trade back far enough that the counting had simply stopped being the point somewhere along the way — back past his own teacher, and hers, and further still, back to a particular valley in Italy, where the instrument itself had first taken the shape it still mostly kept, in workshops not unlike this one, smelling, he had always assumed, of more or less the same wood and the same glue. Nobody in his family had ever been able to say with any confidence how many pairs of hands stood between him and that valley. At some point, several generations back, someone had apparently decided the number mattered less than the fact of the line itself continuing to hold, and everyone since had quietly agreed with that decision by simply never restarting the count.

He built by hand. Not exclusively — he was not a fool about it, and the shop had precision tools tucked into drawers his grandmother would not have recognized — but the part that mattered, the part that decided whether a violin would end up merely correct or actually alive, that part still happened the slow way, with his own hands, the way it always had. Nobody asked him to. Nobody had asked anyone in his family to, in longer than anyone still living could account for. He did it because the alternative — asking the system to simply produce one, correctly, instantly, indistinguishable by any measurement from something that had taken him half a year — would have meant admitting that the seven months were decoration. He did not believe they were decoration. He had built his whole life on not believing that, and he was old enough now that changing his mind about it would have cost him more than he had left to spend.

He remembered, with the specific clarity certain memories keep long after the surrounding years have gone soft, standing at this same bench at perhaps nine years old, watching his mother thin a top plate down by hand, shaving so fine the curls came away nearly transparent, holding each one up to the window afterward to check the light passed through evenly before setting it aside. He had asked her, once, why she didn't just measure it instead of checking the light — there were tools for that, even then. She had said, without looking up from the wood, that a measurement told you what the thickness was. It didn't tell you what it would sound like in November, or after a decade of somebody's hands warming it every evening, or the day the person who'd been given it as a child finally stopped being afraid of it. The light told her more than the number did, she said, and she had never once, in the whole rest of her life, given him a better reason than that for anything, and he had never once needed one.

The instruments themselves were free, in the sense that everything was free. That had never been the part that interested him. What interested him was the conversation before the wood was ever cut — the part where he asked, plainly, without performing suspicion, whether the person standing in his shop actually intended to learn, or whether they wanted the having of an instrument rather than the becoming of a player. Most people found the question strange. Some found it faintly insulting, as though wanting a beautiful object were itself a character flaw he was accusing them of. He did not mind either reaction. He minded only the ones who lied to him about it, and he had gotten, over the years, uncomfortably good at telling which ones those were.

He always asked, once the instrument was finished and gone home with them, to come back and show him. Not to inspect. To see. There was a difference, and he had learned to make the invitation sound like the second thing even when some part of him meant the first.

There had been an old man, the previous spring, who had come in wanting a mandolin at an age most people would have considered past the point of starting anything new. Anselm had asked him the same question he asked everyone. The old man had said, without hesitation, that he did not expect to be any good at it, ever, and that this was rather the point — he had spent his whole life, until now, only ever doing the things he already knew he could do well, competent, reliable, finished before he'd even begun them, and he wanted, for whatever years he had left, to be bad at something on purpose, patiently, for no reason except that it was his to be bad at. Anselm had built him the instrument gladly, and when the old man came back four months later to show him, he had been, in fact, still quite bad at it — but he had played two full minutes of something recognizable, badly, with his own uncertain hands, and had grinned the entire time like a man who had gotten away with something. Anselm still thought of that afternoon more often than most.

---

The mother had come in on an ordinary afternoon, months earlier, asking for a violin for her daughter. Twelve, she'd said. Sincere, she'd said, before he'd even asked — anticipating the question, which he'd taken, at the time, as a good sign. He'd built it the way he built all of them, slow, uninterrupted, the top plate carved thin enough in the right places that it would breathe rather than merely resonate. Seven months, start to finish, which was on the longer end for him but not unusually so. He had sent word when it was ready. The mother had come to collect it alone, thanked him with what seemed like genuine warmth, and that had been the last he'd heard until he reached out himself, the way he always did, to ask how the girl was getting on.

She'd like to come show him, the mother said. She was excited to.

He liked that word. Excited, not proud, not finished — excited implied a thing still in motion, still becoming, which was the only state he had ever really wanted his instruments to arrive into.

---

The girl came in holding her mother's hand, which she released almost immediately upon entering, the particular gesture of someone twelve years old reminding the room she did not need to be led anywhere. Behind her came the robot — hers, evidently, the kind built companionably rather than for labor, the kind that walked with an unhurried, faintly deferential gait that Anselm had never quite gotten used to, though he had seen enough of them by now that the not-getting-used-to-it had become its own kind of familiar.

"She's been working so hard," the mother said, and there was no performance in it that he could detect. Whatever pride was about to be on display in the next few minutes, this part of it, at least, was real.

"Show him," the girl said, to the robot, not to Anselm, and stepped back half a pace to make room.

The robot lifted the violin — his, the one he had spent seven months on, the wood he could have picked out of a hundred others blind — and played.

It was, by any honest measure he had, genuinely beautiful. Not merely correct, not the flat competence he half-expected and had half-hoped for, unkindly, somewhere in the private part of himself he generally kept locked during working hours. It breathed. It found the phrase and let it settle exactly where it needed to settle, and for four full minutes Anselm stood in his own shop and listened to an instrument he had not built produce music more alive than anything he could remember hearing from an actual student's hands in a very long time.

The girl was beaming by the end of it. Not embarrassed. Not the careful, testing look of a child unsure whether an adult would approve — she looked, unmistakably, like someone who had done something clever and wanted it recognized as such.

"I taught it," she said. "The pieces, I mean. I found them, and I showed it how they should go, and it just — got it. Right away, basically. So I don't have to practice for hours and still sound bad. It just already sounds right."

Her mother's hand had found her shoulder at some point during this, and the pride on the mother's face was not, Anselm could see clearly, pride in the violin, or in the music. It was pride in the exact thing the girl had just described — the noticing, the teaching, the cleverness of having found a way around the part that was supposed to be hard.

"That's — " Anselm started, and did not finish, because he did not yet know which sentence he was reaching for.

---

"Would you play something for me," he said instead, once he'd found something steadier to stand on. "Just a little. Whatever you've got, even if it isn't much yet."

The girl looked at him the way she might have looked at someone suggesting she walk somewhere the transit already went. Not offended. Genuinely puzzled. "It already played it, though. Better than I would."

"I know it did. I heard it. I'd like to hear you, anyway."

"Why? If it already sounds right?"

He did not have an answer ready that he trusted himself to say kindly, so he let the silence sit a moment instead of filling it badly. The girl, in the absence of a real reason, did as children generally do when adults ask something without explaining it well enough to earn immediate cooperation — she deferred, without quite refusing, to the person who might actually have the standing to insist. She looked at her mother.

Her mother did not insist either. "Maybe another time," she said, gently, to Anselm rather than to the girl, in the specific tone of someone closing a door without wanting it to sound like a door closing.

He let it close. He had learned, slowly, over enough years of this exact small negotiation playing out in slightly different shapes, that pushing past that particular kind of gentle refusal cost more than it ever purchased.

"It took me seven months to build that," he said instead, nodding at the violin still in the robot's hands, and he heard, as he said it, that it had come out a little more plainly than he'd intended — not sharp, he didn't think, but not fully hidden either. A small, honest complaint wearing the coat of a simple fact. "By hand. Most of it."

"It's really good," the girl said, generously, meaning it as a compliment, which somehow landed harder than if she hadn't.

"I could teach you," he said. "Properly. However long it takes. Just you and the instrument and however many months it needs."

"I don't think I want to do it that way," the girl said, without cruelty, the plain honesty of someone who had not yet learned to soften a true thing for the sake of the person hearing it. "This way I still get to play it. I just don't have to be bad at it first."

There was nothing in that sentence he could argue with on its own terms, which was, he thought, very nearly the whole problem.

---

She went outside a few minutes later, restless the way children get restless in rooms where the adults have shifted into a register she had already lost interest in, the robot following at its same unhurried pace. The mother lingered by the door.

"I don't think she understood what you were actually asking her," she said, once the girl was out of earshot. "I don't say that to excuse it. I think I mean it as a fact."

"I think you're right," Anselm said. "I don't think that makes it easier to hear."

"No." The mother looked back toward the door, toward the shape of her daughter through the glass, patient and unbothered, waiting. "I was proud of her, in there. I want to say that honestly, because I think you saw it and I'd rather not pretend you didn't. I was proud. She found a real solution to a real problem and she did it herself, and I don't think that's nothing."

"I don't think it's nothing either."

"But I keep thinking about your face. While it was playing." She said it without accusation, more like someone reporting a measurement she hadn't asked to take. "You didn't look like a man listening to something beautiful. You looked like a man at a funeral being told how peaceful the deceased looked."

He almost laughed, despite everything, at the precision of that. "That's fair."

"In a few days I'll bring it back," she said. "She won't even notice. She'll have found something else by then, she always does. I promise. So you can find it a proper home."

"You don't have to promise that."

"I know I don't." She was already turning toward the door as she said it, toward her daughter and the small, patient machine waiting beside her. "I'm promising it anyway."

She smiled at him, kindly, the smile of someone who understood exactly what had happened in the room behind her — the months, the wood, the robot, the daughter who had never once picked up the instrument herself — and did not need any of it explained to her twice.

He watched them go — the girl already talking, animated, to something that had no real need to hear her and would listen with total, uncomplaining patience regardless. He stood in the doorway a while after the small bell above it had stopped moving, listening to his own shop's ordinary quiet, the stove still going, the smell of glue and shaved spruce settling back over everything the way it always did once a customer had gone.

He thought, standing there, of his mother again, holding a shaving up to the window to check the light. She had never once, in her whole life as far as he knew, been asked the question that afternoon had just handed him — nobody had ever offered her a version of the craft that skipped the seven months and kept the sound. The question had simply not existed yet, in her lifetime, in any form she would have needed to answer. He was not certain, standing in the doorway now, whether that had made her luckier than him or simply earlier, standing on a piece of ground that had not yet finished shifting underneath it. He suspected, uncomfortably, that it might only have been the second thing, and that the ground had been moving the whole time, long before he was old enough to feel it start.

He did not know, standing there, whether he had lost something that afternoon or simply watched something finish the ordinary, unremarkable way most things eventually finish — not stolen, not broken, just quietly outgrown by someone who had never fully needed it in the first place. He suspected, if he was honest with the version of himself that came out mostly at closing time, that it did not much matter which of those two things it actually was. Either way, the violin was going home in its case, unplayed by the hands it had been built for, and either way, he was going to get up tomorrow and cut spruce for someone else's, the same slow way, for reasons he was no longer entirely sure he could defend to anyone who asked him plainly why it still mattered.
