# Second Nature
## Cuttings

*Reading time: approximately 13 minutes*

---

The workbench had a groove on the left edge where his forearm rested when he soldered, and a stain on the right that had given up being anything but permanent. Nobody had taught him to keep a workbench. He'd built this one the way he built everything — badly, a dozen times, until one of the bad versions stopped being bad.

The shoulder joint had beaten him for three weeks. Today it stopped.

He'd quit trying to force one hinge to do the whole job and split the rotation across two smaller joints instead, each one carrying a share. He tightened the last screw and ran the sequence. The arm swept its full range. No catch. No stutter at the edges, where every earlier version had caught for exactly a tenth of a second before continuing — small enough that most people wouldn't have called it a flaw. He ran it again. A third time, slower, watching the seam where the two joints handed the motion between them.

Nothing announced itself. That was what finished meant. Not that it worked — that it had stopped drawing attention to the fact that it worked.

He sat back. His hands opened and closed once against the bench edge, out of habit, the way they did when there was suddenly nothing left to hold.

---

He told his AI to log it — actuation range, the two-joint fix, nothing else. No need for anything else. The log needed to be correct, not full.

The finished arm sat on the bench. He looked at it the way he looked at anything that had just stopped needing him, then looked past it, toward the window, where the plant had been sitting the entire three weeks without once asking for his attention.

He looked at it properly now. The stem bent toward the glass in one smooth curve. No hinge anywhere in it. No single point where the bending happened and the rest stayed straight — the whole length did a small share of the work, all the way along, and from three feet away it read as one deliberate gesture. He'd spent three weeks building two joints to fake what this thing did with none.

He got up, crossed the room, and pressed a finger lightly against the curve, near where it bent sharpest. Nothing to feel. It wasn't that kind of thing.

*How's it do that,* he said. *No joint.*

---

The answer came in layers. Water pressure inside the cells, first — what kept a stem stiff or let it droop. Then growth: one side of the stem lengthening faster than the other in response to light, so the whole thing curved not because any part of it decided to bend, but because the two sides grew at different speeds and the difference became a curve. Then the actual mechanism doing the redistributing — a hormone moving toward the shaded side, telling those cells to stretch further.

He didn't take the words in as words. He never did. He turned the shape of the explanation over until it stopped being a sentence and became something he could hold still and look at from more than one side. Two sides of a stem, growing at two speeds. No hinge required, because nothing was ever asked to do all the bending in one spot.

*Does it know it's doing it.*

The answer took longer this time, and hedged more — no single site, no control center, nothing anyone had found that could be called a decision. The whole business ran on local rules that added up to something that looked like intention from the outside.

He liked that answer better. Real uncertainty always got more out of him than a clean one did. A clean answer closed a question. This one left it open, and open was where he did his best thinking — when he did anything that could honestly be called thinking, instead of just building until something worked.

He picked the finished arm back up, rotated it once at the shoulder, and set it down beside the plant. For a second both of them sat in the same patch of light — one bending because two sides disagreed by degrees, the other turning because two joints had learned to pass a motion between them without a seam.

*Anyone building with it,* he said. *Not studying it. Using the plant as the part that does the work. Not a sensor stuck to it so someone else can watch.*

Some. Not much. A short list — living tissue fused into robotic frames that grew and adapted together over time. A single plant, once, wired straight into a small wheeled machine, its own weak signals amplified enough to actually steer it toward light. Something smaller, further along the same idea, where a robot carried moss and shaped its own behavior around what the moss needed.

Not many. Not nothing.

He went quiet in a way that was slightly different from how he'd been quiet a minute earlier. Nobody but the AI was around to notice the difference. It noticed anyway.

---

He didn't go looking for a plan after that. He went looking for room.

*Can a greenhouse go off my room. Small. Enough to actually grow things. Not a windowsill.*

The structural check came back fast — exterior wall, load, materials, a rough build estimate. It could be done. Anything attached to the house would need his parents' approval first.

He hadn't been thinking about his parents at all until that sentence. Most of what he built existed, finished or close to it, before anyone else heard a word about it — the asking, when there was asking, came after. But the house wasn't only his to build onto, and he understood that the same way he understood most rules nobody had ever explained to him: by running into the edge of it and finding it solid.

His father read the structural estimate off the screen, nodded, said yes — barely a comment either way, the way he'd said yes to enough of what came out of that room by now that the questions had mostly stopped making sense to ask. Then he glanced over and had to step around a sock, half-kicked-off, sitting in the middle of the floor exactly where someone had simply stopped needing it.

His mother didn't know yet.

He noticed that the way he noticed most things that weren't directly in front of him — briefly, at the edge, without much weight on it. He was already past it, already thinking about what he wanted to grow inside a space that didn't exist yet. He didn't know what, not really. He knew a windowsill was never going to hold it.
