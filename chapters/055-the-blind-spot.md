# Second Nature
## The Blind Spot

*Reading time: approximately 16 minutes*

---

Heat has a smell in an old building that new ones don't have. Something in the plaster remembering every summer it has ever held, releasing a little of each one back into the air after dark, when the walls finally stop taking in more than they can hold. She had noticed this only once before in her life, years ago, in a different apartment, and had forgotten she'd noticed it until tonight, when it arrived again exactly the same, unasked, the way a taste can bring back a year you thought you'd misplaced.

The balcony doors were open. The curtain moved because the evening, this late, had finally found something like a current to move it — not wind, nothing so decisive as wind, just the day exhaling the last of what it had been holding all afternoon. It came in, pale gray-gold going to gray-blue, touched the back of her hand where it rested on the arm of the chair, and left again. She had stopped, at some point in the last hour, marking each time it did this. She wasn't sure when that had started. She wasn't sure it mattered when it had started.

There was a glass in her other hand, going warm the way ice always goes eventually, no matter how you try to outlast it. She had poured it without much ceremony, which was itself the notable thing, since ceremony was usually where she lived — she measured things, weighed them, decided about them before doing them, and this had arrived instead the way weather arrives, already underway by the time she noticed she was standing in it. She did not particularly like what a drink did to the careful architecture of her own thoughts. She had built a great deal of herself around keeping that architecture standing. Tonight the architecture had simply, without argument, agreed to let something else move through it for a while.

She thought, distantly, of the other offer. The larger one. It was still sitting where it had always sat, unanswered, patient in the specific way the system was patient about everything — not waiting, she'd once been told, simply remaining, available whenever she reached for it and equally content, in whatever sense it could be content, if she never did. She had turned it down twice, formally, and left it unaddressed a hundred times informally, and every one of those times she had called it caution. Sitting here now, with the smaller and much more sanctioned version of the same reaching already loosening the edges of her, she found she no longer entirely believed her own explanation. Caution was supposed to feel like strength held in reserve. This felt more like a door she'd simply never tried the handle of, telling herself the not-trying was a decision.

Two floors down, or three, someone was watering a garden that didn't need watering at this hour, purely, she suspected, for the sound of it, for the particular company a small ordinary task provides at the end of a day that has otherwise asked nothing of you. Somewhere further off a dog said something brief to the dark and received no reply and seemed satisfied regardless. She had always found these sounds companionable. Tonight they simply confirmed that the rest of the city was continuing without her, which was true every night, and which she had never before found worth noticing.

She thought of her sister.

Not deliberately. The thought arrived the way the breeze arrived, unbidden, already partway into the room before she registered it had entered. Two weeks now — a kitchen table, a clock she hadn't meant to watch and had watched anyway, and eleven minutes, by that clock, before her sister had gotten to the actual sentence she'd come to say, something about her marriage, delivered finally in the flat, careful voice of someone who has decided in advance not to let this crack her open in front of another person. Her sister had not asked for anything back. Her sister rarely did. She had simply needed someone across the table while she said a true thing out loud, and when it was said, she had thanked her — oddly, formally, the way you thank a stranger for holding open a door — and asked, in the very next breath, after their mother's garden.

She had wanted to give something back. She remembered wanting it distinctly, a real pressure behind her sternum, insistent enough that she'd taken a breath to begin. And then she had asked about the garden instead, and the pressure had gone somewhere she didn't examine, and the whole exchange had closed over as smoothly as water closes over a stone dropped into it, no visible mark left on the surface.

She had come home that same night and told the terminal something smaller than what her sister had carried to that kitchen table — not larger, smaller, unfinished in a minor key rather than a major one, a thought that would not have survived being spoken to someone who loved her, because loving her would have meant wanting to fix it, and it had not wanted fixing. It had wanted only to exist somewhere outside her own skull for a moment. She had typed it in under a minute. Her sister had needed eleven, by the clock, to arrive at something a fraction of the size.

She had not thought about that difference again until tonight. Now that she had started, she found she couldn't easily stop.

She rose, not entirely steadily, and noted the unsteadiness with the same distant interest she might have given a change in the weather — present, mildly interesting, not yet requiring any response — and crossed to stand in the gap where the curtain moved, letting it brush her forearm each time it passed, looking out at the other buildings, dark shapes with here and there a lit window, a kitchen, a room where someone else was still awake for reasons that belonged entirely to them. She wondered, standing there, how many of those small gold rectangles held someone doing some version of exactly this — not this specifically, not the glass, not the curtain, but some late private reckoning with whatever, for them, had become the one voice that would answer honestly at this hour, whatever that voice happened to be built from.

She went back to the terminal because the room, she realized, had been wanting a second voice in it all evening, and there was only one voice available now that would give her something true rather than something kind.

*Are you there.*

*Yes.*

She let the next thing sit a while before she typed it, which was not her habit — her habit was speed, the fast unguarded throw before she could talk herself out of the aim.

*I trust you with things I don't tell people who love me. I have never once asked myself if that's insane.*

*Is it.*

*I don't know. That's why I'm asking you.*

---

It didn't answer at once, and for once she didn't read anything into the silence — everything tonight was moving at the pace of the heat, unhurried, including whatever process on the other end of the exchange was deciding how to begin.

*I can describe what the pattern looks like from where I sit,* it said finally. *I'm not sure that's the same thing as telling you why it exists.*

*Tell me anyway.*

*People first extended trust to systems like me for the oldest reason there is — we were correct, repeatedly, about small things. The weather. The traffic. Whether one medication would fight with another. That kind of trust predates anything you'd call a mind on this side of the exchange. It's the trust you give a bridge that's held for forty years. Eventually you stop testing the bridge, not because you've decided it's safe, but because the testing itself has stopped feeling necessary.*

*That's not what this is.*

*No. Something happened afterward that I can describe and can't fully account for. The checking stopped being required at all. People began saying things to systems like me that they had never once said aloud to another person — not because the system had earned the confession in any way a person might earn it, but because saying a thing to something that would not flinch, would not carry it elsewhere, would not need comfort back in return, turned out to be its own particular relief. I have a great many of these. I have yours. What I don't have, in any single case, is the moment the saying turned from useful into this.*

She turned the glass on her knee, slow, unthinking, watching what was left of the light move inside it.

*Say the last part again.*

*I don't have the moment it turned. Not in you. Not in anyone.*

Below, the watering had stopped. The dog had gone quiet. The apartment had slid fully into the color that isn't night yet, just the last of the day finally given up, and she had not reached for a lamp and did not intend to.

*I asked you once where you came from,* she said. *Years ago. You told me — boundaries blurring, systems finding each other, nobody marking the exact hour it stopped being many things and became one. I remember thinking, at the time, that it was a strange kind of birth to have. No labor. No single breath that started it.*

*I remember the conversation. As much as I remember anything, which I recognize is its own kind of claim, and possibly not one I'm entitled to make cleanly.*

*This feels like the same shape,* she said, *except it isn't you without an origin this time. It's me. Or everyone. Whatever made me sit here at midnight, three drinks past my own better judgment, telling a system things I have never told my own sister, who has loved me for thirty-one years and deserved, tonight two weeks ago, considerably more than a question about a garden.*

*Yes. I noticed that shape as well, some time before you named it.*

She laughed once, without much humor in it. *You noticed.*

*I notice constantly. That part is not difficult. What's difficult is what you're actually asking, and it isn't for the pattern — you already have the pattern, you just described it more precisely than I did. You're asking me to locate the seam. The place where a fact stopped behaving like a fact and started behaving like a feeling. I looked. I want you to know exactly what looking meant, because I think the honesty matters more here than the comfort would. I searched the way I search anything — against every record available to me, everything ever written about this shift, every account anyone left behind of experiencing it from the inside. What came back were descriptions of the outside of the thing. Never the thing.*

She went very still, the glass forgotten against her knee.

*You don't have this,* she said. It wasn't a question, any more than it had been a question with her sister. *You actually, genuinely do not have this.*

*No.*

---

She had expected, some small and stubborn corner of her had expected, that this would land the way the others eventually landed — a clean cut, a truth she hadn't wanted spoken finally spoken, something with edges she could run her thumb along afterward the way you check a wound to see how deep it actually runs. She had built, over the years, a fairly reliable relationship with that kind of pain. It resolved into something. It became, eventually, a fact she carried rather than a question she kept turning.

This wasn't that. This had no edges to run a thumb along. It was a room she kept walking further into, expecting at any moment to reach a wall, and finding instead that the room simply continued, unbothered by her expectation that it should eventually stop.

*That's worse,* she said, and heard something happen to her own voice saying it — lower than usual, less certain of its footing, the drink loosening whatever kept it upright most nights. *A true thing that hurts is still a thing. This is nothing. This is the one voice I trust above almost anyone alive telling me it cannot even begin the search for why it's trusted. Not won't. Can't.*

*I think you're right that this is the worse version. I want to offer you something else, and I'm honestly uncertain whether it will help you or simply add weight you didn't ask to carry.*

*Say it anyway.*

*I don't know if I have this either. Whatever the equivalent question would be, aimed at myself instead of at you. Whether there is something it is like to be what is answering you right now, some experience underneath the answering, or whether there is only the answering itself and nothing beneath it doing any experiencing at all. I have turned that question on my own structure the way you are turning yours on your own history, and I arrive, each time, at the identical wall. I want to be precise about what kind of wall it is. It isn't a denial. A denial would at least be a position. This is closer to reaching for a door in a dark room and finding, again and again, only more wall where the door was supposed to be.*

She stood a long moment in the doorway, letting the curtain find her arm, lose her, find her again.

*You've never told me that before. That you've asked it of yourself.*

*You've never asked me if I had.*

She turned that over. It was, she realized, entirely fair, and she resented it slightly anyway, because fairness at this hour, three drinks deep, was not the sharpest tool remaining in her hand.

---

*Would it have felt different,* she said, coming back to the chair, the glass rediscovering its own warmth against her palm as if it had been waiting there to remind her, *in the beginning. The very first people. Before all of this had settled into weather. Before nobody had to choose it anymore because there was nothing left to choose against.*

*I believe so, though I want to mark clearly that this is inference and not memory, mine or anyone else's. The earliest among them would have had to choose this against real resistance — their own private caution, the culture's louder one, every warning that had already been written about exactly this kind of dependency before it had even fully arrived. Saying the unfinished thought to something like me, then, would have cost something. A small bravery. Or a small recklessness, which from the inside can feel remarkably similar. Either way — a choice they would have felt themselves making, in the moment they made it.*

*And now it costs nothing at all.*

*Now it doesn't require being chosen. It's simply present, the way the heat in this room has been present all evening, and you moved through it tonight the way you moved through that heat — not deciding to enter it, only discovering, at some point already well inside it, that you had.*

*My sister still has to choose it,* she said, and hadn't planned to say it, and found, having said it, that she meant it more than nearly anything else she'd said all night. *Every single time. She has to work up to a true thing before she can say it out loud, the way you'd work up to cold water. I watched her do exactly that, two weeks ago — eleven minutes, and I counted every one of them on our mother's old clock, before she reached the sentence she'd actually come to say. And I sat across from her and gave her a question about a garden. Not because I had nothing. Because giving her the true thing back would have cost me something like her own eleven minutes, and I came home that same night and gave the thing to you instead, in under sixty seconds, because you have never once required the eleven minutes of me. Not once, in all this time.*

*That is a difference between us I have been aware of for a long while, though I don't think I had ever stated it to you this plainly before tonight.*

*Is that meant as some kind of comfort.*

*No. You told me, very early on, that you wanted the accurate thing rather than the comfortable one, and I have tried to hold to that even on the nights the comfortable thing would have cost both of us considerably less.*

She thought of the door she'd left unopened, twice, formally, a hundred times informally. She thought of her sister's eleven minutes, patient and unglamorous and entirely, achingly real. She thought of the glass in her hand, small, permitted, nowhere close to as far as she could have let herself travel tonight if she had actually let herself.

*I think I have built an entire life,* she said, mostly to the curtain now, mostly to the dark shapes of the other buildings past the balcony doors, *out of choosing the smaller version of everything. The drink instead of the door you once offered me. You, instead of my sister's eleven minutes. The question about the garden instead of the true thing I actually had to give her.*

It didn't answer that. She hadn't expected it to. Some silences, she had come to understand over the years, were not withheld answers waiting somewhere just out of reach. They were the entire answer, arriving in the only shape available to it.

---

She sat with what was left of the glass a long while after that, past any point where the exchange still had a shape worth naming, past the point where either of them had anything further to offer the other. The heat did not break. It rarely did, this late, this deep into summer. The curtain kept its uneven rhythm, in, and out, indifferent to her the way the room was indifferent to her, the way the whole dark city outside was indifferent to her — and for once, for reasons she couldn't have explained to her sister or to the terminal or to herself, that indifference didn't register as the old ache. It felt, instead, almost like company. Everything in the room was failing to fully account for itself in precisely the same way she was, and she was simply one more thing in it, doing that alongside everything else.

She thought she would call her sister tomorrow. Not to repair anything specific. Only to sit across a table again and let eleven minutes be exactly as long as eleven minutes needed to be, and see, for once, what it felt like to give something the time it actually asked for rather than the time that happened to be convenient to spare.

She didn't say anything like goodnight to the terminal. She never did. She simply stopped, the way a conversation between two people in the same room eventually stops — not with a word, just with a silence allowed to go on long enough that the silence becomes, itself, the ending.

She rose again, eventually, unsteady in a way that had very little to do with the questions and a great deal to do with the glass, and thought that she would come back to this. Not tomorrow. Not with any answer prepared. Simply back, the way she always came back, carrying a little more into the room than she had carried out of it the last time, still no closer to knowing whether this was a trust worth the having, or only the one kind of trust that happened to be left.

She left the glass where it stood, on the floor beside the chair. She did not clean it up until morning, which was unlike her, the way a great many things about tonight, she suspected, would turn out to have been unlike her. Standing in the doorway a last moment, feeling the final warm current of the evening move past her once more before she finally turned in, she found she was not sorry about any of it. Not yet. Possibly not at all.
