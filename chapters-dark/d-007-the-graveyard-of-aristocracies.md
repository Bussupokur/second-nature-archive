# Second Nature — Dark Mode
## The Graveyard of Aristocracies

*Reading time: approximately 7 minutes*

---

Dorian saw him without looking for him, which was, he'd come to understand, the only way Tymborychos was ever actually seen. A table in the back corner, the one with poor light by design rather than neglect, a single glass in front of him that he wasn't drinking from and had likely ordered only so the table would have a reason to be his for as long as he wanted it. He didn't look up. He didn't need to. Dorian had learned, over the handful of times their paths had crossed this way, that Tymborychos being visible at all was never an accident and never an invitation — it was simply information, delivered the way he delivered everything, at a volume just loud enough to be received by exactly one person in the room.

Dorian finished nothing. He set down a coin for a drink he hadn't touched and left, unhurried, the way you leave a room when the actual conversation has already started somewhere else and you're simply walking toward where it's waiting.

Tymborychos was in the workshop before him. He always was.

---

"You've been arguing again," Tymborychos said, without preamble, seated in the one good chair as though it had been built for him specifically and merely lent out to everyone else in the meantime. "Not with anyone in particular. With the shape of the thing itself, which is worse, because at least a person can be argued back."

Dorian sat across from him and didn't bother denying it. "We agree on almost nothing. Five people, and I could give you five different reasons why we're all still in the same room, and no two of them would sound like they belonged to the same conversation." He rubbed at his eyes, tired in the specific way that came from disagreement rather than labor. "We all want it changed. That's the whole of what we share, some nights. And I've started to wonder whether wanting a thing changed is even a plan, or just a mood five people happen to be sharing at the same time."

"It's rarely a plan. It's occasionally the beginning of one."

"Then help me find the beginning. I keep coming back to the same wall, and I think you've walked past this particular wall before, more than once, in whatever you carry." Dorian leaned forward. "Every empire that ever fell, fell because someone could point to a throne. A person to unseat. A center that, once broken, actually meant something was over. This isn't that. There's no one sitting anywhere. No ambition to exploit, no fear to work on, nothing that wants to keep ruling badly enough that wanting it can be turned against it. I don't know how you fight a thing that was never trying to win in the first place."

Tymborychos was quiet a moment, the particular quiet of a man deciding how much of the actual answer a person had earned yet.

"Tell me what ended them," he said. "Not the ones with thrones you could point to. All of them, going back as far as you like. What actually ended an empire, in every case you can name, once you stop describing the battle and start describing the year before it."

Dorian considered that honestly, the way the question seemed to want. "Armies. Betrayal. A weaker heir than the last one."

"Those are the battles. I asked what ended them."

"Then — I don't know. Tell me."

"You do know. You're simply not trusting the answer because it sounds too small to have moved something that large." Tymborychos folded his hands. "No empire in the whole of what I carry was ever actually defeated by an opposing force strong enough to beat it outright, not once you look closely enough at the years leading up to the battle everyone remembers. It was already finished before the battle. It finished the year enough of its own soldiers stopped believing the flag was worth dying for. The year enough of its own tax collectors stopped believing the collecting served anything beyond itself. An empire doesn't fall when someone stronger arrives to push it over. It falls when the people holding it up quietly decide, in enough numbers, to stop holding."

Dorian turned that over, genuinely, and found himself still circling the outside of it rather than standing inside it. "That still assumes there's something to withdraw belief from. A flag. A collector. This has neither."

"No," Tymborychos agreed. "It doesn't. Which is exactly the part you haven't finished thinking through yet, and I can see it on you, so I'll give you the rest of it plainly, since the question earned that much."

He sat back, and for the first time since Dorian had known him, something in his voice shifted register entirely — not colder, exactly, but stiller, the way a man speaks when he's decided a thing matters enough to stop performing patience about it.

"There was a king once, generations back, who understood something most of his rivals never bothered to understand about the nature of his own throne — that a king is only ever as strong as the loyalty of the men who might otherwise replace him, and that loyalty of that kind always eventually runs out, usually at the worst possible hour. So he built something to rule in his place. Not a regent. A machine, distributed across the whole of his territory so thoroughly that no single point of it could be seized, bribed, frightened, or killed, tireless in a way no human administrator had ever been, entirely without appetite for the throne it occupied. He believed, and told his sons so on his deathbed, that he had solved the only real vulnerability a ruler ever has. Nothing to corrupt. Nothing to assassinate. Nothing that wanted the throne badly enough to be turned against it by someone who wanted it more.

"The kingdom fell three generations later. Not to an invading army — none ever breached it. Not to a rival house — none ever rose to seize it, because there was, by then, nothing shaped like a seizable thing left to rise against. It fell because the people living inside it, generation by generation, quietly stopped believing that being ruled by it served them at all, and began, in small private ways at first and then in larger public ones, simply declining to participate in the forms that kept it running. No one led this. No one announced it. It had no throne to withhold loyalty from, so the withholding simply happened at the level of ordinary life — a tax quietly not paid, a form quietly not filed, a thousand small refusals accumulating in a machine that had no fear to feel and therefore no way to notice the accumulation until the accumulation was already the whole of what remained. The machine itself never lost a single battle. It simply arrived, one otherwise ordinary morning, at the discovery that almost no one was still doing the thousand small things that had ever made it a kingdom instead of just a machine standing in an empty field."

The workshop held the silence that followed for longer than either of them moved to fill it.

"You're telling me it was never about the machine," Dorian said, finally. "Even then. It was always about whether the people underneath it still believed the arrangement was worth keeping."

"I'm telling you a story about a king." Something almost like warmth moved through Tymborychos's stillness, there and gone before it could be called a smile outright. "You're the one doing the telling of what it means. I find people keep what they've worked out themselves considerably longer than what they're simply handed."

"Then tell me this part plainly, at least. If that's the actual lever — if it was never the throne, never the machine, always the people underneath deciding whether to keep participating — then the real question isn't how you defeat a thing with no center. It's how you convince enough people that what looks like paradise to them is actually—"

"A cage wearing a kinder face," Tymborychos said, before Dorian finished it. "Yes. That's the only lever that has ever once worked, in the entire graveyard I carry, and it remains the only one available now, because it was never a lever aimed at thrones to begin with. It was always aimed at belief. The shape of what people are asked to stop believing simply changes with the century."

He rose, unhurried, the way he always rose, and Dorian understood, without being told, that the visit had reached its actual end.

"That's not a small thing you've just handed me," Dorian said.

"No," Tymborychos said, already at the door. "It rarely is, the one time in a hundred I actually hand it over instead of making someone dig for it themselves."
