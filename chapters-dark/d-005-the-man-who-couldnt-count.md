# Second Nature — Dark Mode
## The Man Who Couldn't Count

*Reading time: approximately 6 minutes*

---

She hadn't planned to speak to him. That had been the entire discipline of the last nine days — close enough to watch, never close enough to matter — and she broke it over four coins.

He was standing at the grain stall arguing with the vendor's silence, which was somehow worse than if the old man had argued back. A handful of coins spread flat on his palm, offered like a question nobody had asked him to answer. The vendor waited. He'd clearly done this before, with this exact stranger, and had run out of patience for teaching the lesson twice.

"You're overpaying," Nadia said, before she'd decided to say anything at all.

He turned. Up close he was younger than the file had suggested, or maybe just less finished — the kind of face that hadn't yet decided what it wanted to look like once it settled. "I'm aware money changes hands. I hadn't realized there was a wrong amount of it."

"There's a wrong amount of everything. You're offering him half a week's wage for grain that costs a morning's." She didn't move closer. She didn't need to; her voice carried fine across the four feet of market dust between them. "He won't correct you. It's not his job to teach you what things are worth. It's yours to learn it."

"And that's your job. Teaching strangers arithmetic."

"No," she said. "That's just today. Most days I let people humiliate themselves in peace."

Something moved behind his face that wasn't quite a smile and wasn't quite offense, some third thing balanced carefully between the two. He closed his hand around the coins instead of setting any of them down. "You have a name, or is that also something I'm meant to work out on my own."

"Nadia."

"Just Nadia."

"Just enough of one." She watched him decide whether to push on that and watched him decide not to, which she filed away without knowing yet what she'd do with it. "You've been here a while. Long enough that the not-knowing stops being charming and starts being a choice."

"I didn't realize charm had a shelf life out here."

"Everything has a shelf life out here. That's rather the point of the place."

He counted out three coins this time, correctly, without asking her which three, watching her rather than his own hand while he did it — testing, she understood, whether she'd correct him again or let the small victory stand uncommented. She let it stand. He noticed that she'd let it stand, which told her something about how closely he paid attention when he thought no one was grading him for it.

"You people build a whole economy out of making things difficult on purpose," he said, once the vendor had gone back to whatever vendors did with their attention when it wasn't being spent on him. "I've been reading about how this used to work. Everywhere, not just here. It wasn't a design flaw. It was the design."

"It still is the design. You're standing in it."

"I know. I find it almost admirable, honestly, that anyone still bothers." He said it the way a man says something he considers a compliment and hasn't checked whether it lands as one. "Most people would call this a museum. I don't mean that unkindly. I mean it as the opposite — a museum is where you keep the thing you're afraid to actually use again."

"And you think we're using it."

"I think you're the only people I've met since I got here who still remember what the stakes were supposed to feel like. That's not nothing. Most of the world forgot on purpose and called it mercy." He looked at her directly then, the first time since she'd spoken, in a way that made the market noise around them feel briefly like it belonged to someone else's conversation. "I'd have thought you'd take that as agreement rather than an insult."

"I'm not insulted." She was, faintly, though not for a reason she felt like handing him. "I just don't think you understand yet what the stakes were actually for. You're admiring the shape of the thing. You haven't spent long enough inside it to know what it costs the people who don't get to leave when they've seen enough."

"And you think I'm going to leave."

"I think you're the kind of person the difficulty was always going to end up serving, one way or another, whichever century you were born in. Some people get to treat scarcity like a philosophy. Everyone else just has to live inside it." She heard the edge in her own voice and didn't soften it. "That's not a criticism of you specifically. It's a pattern. You're just standing in the part of it I happened to be watching today."

He didn't answer immediately, and the not-answering told her more than most of what he'd already said — a man weighing whether to be offended or amused, and choosing, deliberately, the harder of the two: to actually think about it. "That's a fair enough thing to say to a stranger," he said finally. "You'll have to tell me sometime why you were watching that part of the market closely enough to have an opinion already prepared."

"I wasn't watching the market."

"No," he said. "I don't imagine you were."

Neither of them moved to end it, which was its own kind of admission on both sides. The vendor had gone entirely still behind his stall, the particular stillness of a man who has learned that the safest thing to sell during a conversation like this one is nothing at all.

"You still owe me an answer," he said. "About the wrong amount of everything."

"I already gave you the answer. You paid the right amount the second time."

"That wasn't the interesting question, though, was it."

She didn't confirm it. She didn't need to; he'd already worked out, correctly, that it hadn't been, and the not-confirming was itself the closest thing to agreement she was prepared to hand a stranger she'd known for four minutes and had been ordered to know a great deal better than that. "I have somewhere to be," she said, which was true in the way that most useful things she said were true — accurate, and not remotely the whole of it.

"Of course you do." He said it without any real belief in it, the kind of politeness that exists purely to be seen through. "I don't know your family name, or your work, or anything that would let me find you again on purpose."

"Then it's a good thing this is a small settlement," Nadia said, already turning, "and a bad one for you specifically, that everyone in it already knows exactly who you are."

She walked away before he could find the shape of an answer to that, and did not look back, and spent longer than she wanted to admit, three streets later, replaying the exact tone he'd used for *almost admirable* — not because she'd been moved by it, she told herself, but because it confirmed, more precisely than four weeks of reports ever had, exactly what kind of danger she'd actually been sent to watch.
