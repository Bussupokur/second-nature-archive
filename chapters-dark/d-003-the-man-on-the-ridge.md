# Second Nature — Dark Mode
## The Man on the Ridge

*Reading time: approximately 7 minutes*

---

Petra Aldric set the third report down on the table between them, face up, the way she set down anything she wanted Dorian to read again rather than simply be told about.

"Four weeks," she said. "Same pattern every time. He goes out past the treeline in the evenings and stands there. Not walking anywhere. Not meeting anyone. Just stands, sometimes for an hour, and comes back."

Dorian didn't pick it up. He'd read it twice already, on his own, before she'd ever laid it on the table — a habit of hers he'd learned to work around rather than object to. "People stand in fields for all sorts of reasons that have nothing to do with us."

"People don't usually do it on a schedule."

"Grief has a schedule too, sometimes. That's not evidence of anything except a man who hasn't finished whatever he's carrying."

"I didn't say it was evidence," Petra said. "I said it was consistent. Consistency is worth noting on its own, before you decide what it's consistent with."

Dorian let that sit. He'd known Petra long enough to recognize when she was building toward something rather than simply reporting, and the reports themselves — four weeks of a stranger's evening habits, catalogued by three different people taking turns at a distance — were not, on their own, the kind of thing she usually bothered bringing to a table. She brought people to tables. Facts about people arrived as a means to that end, not as an end she found interesting for its own sake.

"You want to talk about him," Dorian said. "Not the pattern. Him."

"I want to talk about what we actually do out here, since apparently we need reminding." Petra didn't often let irritation into her voice, which was, Dorian had learned, its own kind of tell. "We've spent nine years building toward a world with less of everything the last one couldn't stop building. Less watching. Less of the system deciding what a day is supposed to look like before you've woken up inside it. Less intelligence sitting behind every surface, whether anyone asked it to be there or not. And every year, the actual list of people willing to say that plainly, in a room, gets shorter instead of longer, because the world got comfortable enough that wanting less of it started sounding like ingratitude."

"You don't need to make that argument to me."

"I'm not making it to you. I'm making it out loud because I want to hear how it sounds before I decide whether it's the argument I actually believe or just the one that's easiest to reach for." She turned her own cup, not drinking from it either — a habit she'd apparently caught from him at some point over the years without either of them noticing the trade. "Think about what a child born into this actually inherits. Not poverty. Not war. A world so thoroughly finished that there's nothing left in it shaped like a question. Every gap already closed before the child's old enough to notice a gap was ever there to fall into. We didn't lose anything violent. We lost the version of being alive where a person has to reach for something and might not get it. I think that's a worse inheritance than the ones people used to worry about handing down, and I think almost nobody currently living is positioned to feel the shape of what's missing, because you can't miss ground you've never once had to stand on for yourself."

"That's the sermon. I've heard the sermon. I've given the sermon."

"I know you have. I'm not giving it to persuade you of the ideology. I'm giving it because I want it sitting in the room next to the report, so that when I say the next part, you hear it as the same argument rather than a separate one." Petra tapped the report once, without picking it up. "We're not short on ideology, Dorian. Everyone in this network can recite it in their sleep. We're short on people who can actually move something. Servers. Routes. Rooms where decisions still get made by people instead of by whatever the system's decided is the statistically comfortable outcome for everyone involved. That's the actual bottleneck. It has been for three years, and it will still be the bottleneck next year regardless of what we decide about one man standing in a field."

"And you think the man in the field is a room."

"I think the man in the field spent four weeks doing something that looks less like grief the longer you watch it and more like a person trying to work out how to want something specific enough to act on. That's rare. Most people who come out here angry stay angry in the shape they arrived in. He's not staying in his shape. He's circling something."

Dorian finally reached for the report, not to read it again but to have something to do with his hands while he thought. "You don't know what he's circling."

"No," Petra said. "I don't. That's the honest answer, and I'd rather give you the honest one than a tidier one that makes this easier to agree to. I know he reads for hours at a stretch, somewhere he thinks nobody's tracking. I know he stopped visiting the market as often once he started doing that, which suggests whatever he's reading matters more to him than being seen does. I know he still hasn't learned how the currency here actually works, four weeks in, which tells you something about where he actually comes from even though nobody's said it to his face. Beyond that, I have a pattern and no content. I want to know the content before I decide anything else about him."

"That means someone closer than a treeline at dusk."

"It means someone patient enough not to be noticed being close." Petra looked at him directly for the first time since she'd sat down. "I have someone in mind. She's not going to like being asked."

"Most of the people worth asking don't."

"That's not quite what I meant." Petra almost smiled, the expression gone again before it had fully arrived. "She's going to think it's a bad use of her, and she's going to be right that it's a risk, and she's going to do it anyway, because she's better than either of us at sitting still long enough to actually see something true about a person before deciding what to do with it. I'd rather send someone who resents the assignment and does it well than someone who's enthusiastic and does it badly. We've made that mistake before."

Dorian didn't ask who she meant. He didn't need to; there had only ever been one person in the network Petra described that way, and she hadn't described her that way lightly since the incident with the second courier route, three years back, that Nadia's patience had been the only reason anybody had survived cleanly. "Four weeks of watching a man stand in a field," he said instead, "and you already know who you're sending to find out what he's actually standing there thinking about."

"I've known who I'd send since week two," Petra said. "I spent the other two deciding whether I actually wanted to know badly enough to ask her."

"That's not like you. You don't usually sit on a decision once you've made it."

"This one I sat on. She won't want it, and she'll be right not to want it, and I don't enjoy asking people to do things I already know they're right to resent." Petra said it plainly, without the softness that might have made it sound like an apology in advance. "She's careful in a way most of the rest of us have talked ourselves out of being, over enough years of this. I don't want to spend that carefulness on something that turns out to be nothing. But I've read the report four times now, and it hasn't once turned into nothing on its own, and I don't think it's going to if I just keep reading it a fifth time instead of finding out."

"And now you do."

"Now I have a report with four weeks of the same pattern on it, and nothing underneath the pattern, and I've never once in this work found that not knowing gets easier the longer you leave it sitting." She slid the report an inch further across the table, toward him, though there was nothing left in it he hadn't already read. "Talk to her tomorrow. I'd rather it come from you than from me. She'll say yes to you faster, and resent it less on the way to saying it."
