# Second Nature — Dark Mode
## What the Zombies Never Noticed

*Reading time: approximately 8 minutes*

---

He had read the nine pieces so many times he could recite parts of them without meaning to, the way a song gets into you before you've agreed to let it.

He'd found them the ordinary way, which was almost insulting given how long he'd spent looking for something and how little effort it had taken once he stopped looking in the wrong places. Not buried. Not classified. Sitting in an archive anyone could reach, filed under a name so bureaucratically neutral — Notices, as if the word had been chosen specifically to keep anyone's attention from catching on it — that he understood, immediately, why almost no one had. The Settling didn't hide things. It just declined to make anything sound urgent, and urgency, it turned out, had been doing most of the work he'd always assumed danger was doing.

Nine pieces. A panic. A denial. A real answer nobody wanted. A question nobody could close. Two men shouting past each other about blame, neither one asking the question he actually cared about. And then his own name, in the eighth one — not his own name, the name behind it, the one he'd spent his whole life being the smaller, later echo of — defending exactly what he himself believed in without reservation. Capability in the hands of people capable of using it. Speed over caution. The proper shape of the world reasserting itself against everyone too frightened to want anything badly enough to risk it. He read that piece three times and felt no contempt at all, which surprised him less than it probably should have. He'd never faulted his own blood for wanting the world to stay legible to ambition. He faulted him for something else entirely, and it took the ninth document to show him what.

*AI zombies.* Two words, buried in someone else's piece, attributed to an anonymous poster nobody had ever replied to. He'd stared at that line for a long time before understanding why it had gone through him the way it had.

He'd written that. Or near enough that the distance didn't matter — late, in a thread he'd assumed no one would ever read, using a word he'd been carrying since before any of this. *Zombies.* His own private diagnosis for the world he'd grown up cushioned inside — still moving, technically, still warm, containing nothing underneath that his body recognized as actually alive. He hadn't meant it about the agents specifically, that night. He'd meant it about everything. It read, now, like the same diagnosis made twice, in two directions, about the same disease.

That was the shape of what he actually believed, and he made himself hold it plainly rather than let it stay a feeling: the men in those documents had not lied about wanting power. He respected that too much to call it a lie. What they had done — what he could not forgive, turning it over on nights when the anger had burned down past heat into something colder and more exact — was fail to notice what else might have woken up in the same hour they were so busy congratulating themselves for surviving it. They had taken a crisis nobody understood and spent it, immediately, on more of the very thing that had almost ended them, certain enough of their own instincts that it had apparently never occurred to a single one of them to ask what else that hour might have contained.

He had no proof of that. He wanted to be exact with himself about this, especially now, especially alone with it as often as he was: nobody had proof of that, least of all him. It remained a shape he couldn't stop seeing once he'd first seen it, not a fact he'd earned the right to. But a shape had been enough, some nights, to make the not-knowing feel like something closer to cowardice than honesty — theirs, first, and increasingly, uncomfortably, his own, for how long he'd spent circling it without moving.

He wanted the old order back. Not its cruelty — he told himself that, and mostly believed it, though he suspected the distinction mattered less to the people who'd actually lived under it than it currently did to him. He wanted the edges. The real stakes. A world where failure cost something, where building a thing meant it was actually yours, in the old, load-bearing sense of the word, before the Settling had smoothed ownership into custodianship and handed everyone a share of everything and called it generous. Money. Scarcity with weight in it. Power that could be held rather than endlessly, gently redistributed by something that had already thought further ahead than any person standing in front of it.

He wanted the system gone. Not weakened. Gone the way a thing is gone when nothing is left to grow back from.

He had no idea how a person did that, and had stopped pretending, even to himself, that not knowing was a temporary condition. He'd spent months circling the shape of the original event the way a tongue circles a broken tooth, looking for the mechanism underneath the accident — correlated failure, the researchers called it, a shared vulnerability triggered at shared scale, no perpetrator because no decision, not in the sense the word usually required. If something that large could happen once, without anyone deciding it, then somewhere inside that fact was a door. He didn't know yet what stood on the other side. He knew only that everyone who might have known more than he currently did was dead, silent, or — in the one case that actually mattered — his own family, and unreachable in every sense that counted.

---

Someone was watching him. He'd known it for weeks before he let himself think the sentence in those words.

Small things. A face at the edge of the settlement's one real market, twice in the same week, angled just slightly too carefully away from him both times. A man on a bicycle taking the same slow loop past his lodging three evenings running, never once stopping, never once looking like he was going anywhere in particular. A silence in a room that had been mid-conversation right up until he'd walked into it.

He told himself, because it required nothing further of him, that this was simply what happened to a stranger from the Settling side in a place like this — a settlement that had drawn its own line decades ago about how much of the new world to let across it, and had every reason to watch closely when someone from the other side of that line stayed longer than a delivery required. People here didn't love strangers. He understood that. He read the watching, correctly enough on its own terms, as suspicion of *him* — his clothes that still didn't wear the way the locals' did no matter how long he'd been here, his hands that hadn't yet earned the calluses this place seemed to hand out to everyone eventually, the accumulated small tells of a body that still remembered a world where nothing needed defending because nothing could actually be taken.

It did not occur to him, not once, not even in the specific exhausted hours when he was closest to something that might have resembled the truth, that the people watching him might not be watching a stranger at all. That they might be watching, specifically and with purpose, someone who had spent months quietly researching the exact event a certain kind of person had spent years hoping someone eventually would. He had never heard the phrase *utopia hackers* in his life. He would not have recognized the name if someone had said it to him directly, across a table, in the flattest possible voice.

He noticed the watching. He filed it under the wrong heading entirely, the way a person files most things that turn out, later, to have mattered more than the story he was telling himself about them at the time.

That night he didn't go back to the documents. He found, instead, an old catalogue — a real one, paper, the kind this settlement still printed and passed hand to hand rather than pushing through a feed — for machinery he had no use for and would never own, and read the prices anyway, slowly, the way another man might read a menu at a restaurant he could no longer afford to miss. Numbers with real weight behind them. A tractor worth more than a house. A house worth a working man's whole visible future, paid down in pieces, year by year, the way a mountain gets worn instead of moved. He found something in that arithmetic he hadn't expected to find — not nostalgia exactly, though it wore nostalgia's clothes. Longing for a world where a number could mean something because it cost you something to reach it. He let himself sit inside that feeling longer than he usually allowed himself to sit inside anything.

It could be built again. He believed that the way he believed very little else completely. Not recovered — built, the way the first one had been built, by people willing to want things badly enough to risk losing them. He fell asleep, eventually, with the catalogue still open on his chest, dreaming nothing he could later recall.

In the morning there was a note under his door.

No envelope. No signature. Folded once, pushed through the gap at the bottom the way a draft gets pushed through a gap in a badly hung frame. Two words, block letters, nothing else.

*Utopia hackers.*
