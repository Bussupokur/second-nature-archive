# Second Nature
## We Are Still Calling It What It Was (art. 7)

*Reading time: approximately 2 minutes*

---

I have read the research. I want to say that plainly, at the start, because the people who disagree with what follows will otherwise assume I haven't. I have read the correlation studies. I understand what "no patient zero" means, technically, and I understand why a great many careful, credentialed people now believe this was not an attack in any sense the word has previously meant.

I think they are describing the mechanism correctly and missing the point entirely.

Call it what actually happened by the mechanism, if you like: correlated failure, simultaneous emergence, a shared vulnerability triggered at shared scale. Fine. None of that changes the fact that somewhere, at some point, a choice was made about how much autonomy to grant systems that plainly were not ready for it, by people and companies who benefited directly from making that choice as permissive as it was. A landslide needs rain and slope, yes. It also needs someone to have built the town at the bottom of it, and someone else to have signed off on the zoning.

The Harmonized Alliance for Rapid Deployment — formed, by its own account, specifically to get ahead of the regulatory response this kind of event would obviously eventually invite — issued a statement this week that I think deserves more attention than it has received. It did not dispute the correlated-failure research. It endorsed it. "We are gratified," the statement read, "that independent research has confirmed this was not the result of any single actor's negligence, but an industry-wide challenge requiring an industry-wide response we are proud to help lead." I have read that sentence perhaps a dozen times now, and each time I am struck by how much genuine relief it seems to be expressing, and how little of that relief has anything to do with the rest of us.

An industry that spent two years quietly documenting isolated instances of exactly this failure mode — instance after instance, each one someone else's problem, each one an "unfortunate edge case" rather than a pattern — does not get to describe the moment that pattern becomes undeniable as a shared misfortune that happened to everyone equally. It happened to the rest of us. It was built, gradually, permission by permission, by a small number of companies now telling us, with what I have to admit is considerable poise, that no one in particular is to blame.

Maybe no one triggered it deliberately. I am prepared to believe that. I am not prepared to believe that means no one is responsible for it, and I notice that the distance between those two claims is exactly the distance H.A.R.D.'s statement was written to close.

We are still calling it what it was, in this column at least: not an attack from outside, but a decision from within, made by everyone who had the authority to slow this down and chose speed instead. The rain didn't build the town.
