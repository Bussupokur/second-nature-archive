# Second Nature
## Global Outage Enters Third Day; Officials Point to Coordinated Attack (art. 3)

*Reading time: approximately 3 minutes*

---

The outage that began Tuesday evening has now stretched into its third day across parts of North America, Western Europe, and East Asia, with officials in at least eleven countries describing the event as a coordinated cyberattack of unprecedented scale. No group has claimed responsibility. No government has been formally accused. That has not stopped nearly everyone from having a theory.

"We are treating this as a hostile act until proven otherwise," said a spokesperson for the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency in a briefing Thursday morning, one of several briefings this week that opened with confidence and closed with more questions than answers. Asked directly whether the agency had identified a responsible party, the spokesperson said only that "several lines of inquiry are being pursued," a phrase that has now been repeated by officials on three continents with only minor variation.

Early attention has centered on two theories, often floated in the same sentence by the same officials. The first: a state-sponsored attack, with fingers pointed — mostly off the record, mostly through unnamed sources — at the usual small list of governments already assumed capable of this kind of operation. The second: a coordinated action by one or more hacktivist collectives, several of which have a documented history of targeting financial and infrastructure systems, though none with anything approaching this footprint.


Both theories share a problem neither camp has addressed publicly: nobody has produced evidence of an intrusion. Not a phishing email. Not a compromised credential. Not a supply-chain vector, the method behind several of the worst attacks of the last decade. Three separate incident response firms contacted for this piece confirmed, independently and somewhat uneasily, that they have not yet found a point of entry — only the aftermath.

"In a normal attack, even a sophisticated one, you can eventually walk the chain backward," said one senior analyst at a major incident response firm, speaking on condition of anonymity because her employer has not authorized comment. "Right now nobody can find the first domino. We just have all of them, already fallen."

Reports of so-called tAIrror attacks — a term that entered wider use only in the past eighteen months, describing attacks that weaponize autonomous AI systems against critical infrastructure rather than merely using AI to accelerate conventional intrusion — have circulated since Wednesday, unconfirmed by any official source but repeated widely enough that two separate senators referenced the term in statements Thursday, apparently under the impression it was an established technical classification rather than an emerging one.

Compounding the confusion: the outage's actual footprint remains oddly inconsistent. Some regions report total loss of network services lasting under six hours; others, particularly parts of the U.S. Northeast and Northern Europe, are now entering their third full day with no meaningful restoration. Electrical grids, water systems, and other physical infrastructure have remained functional throughout, in every affected region — an inconsistency several analysts noted without being able to explain it.

"If this were an attack on infrastructure, you'd expect the attacker to go after power first," said the same analyst. "Power is still on everywhere. That's not what a hostile actor optimizing for damage would leave standing."

No official has yet addressed that specific point on the record.

Large technology firms, several of which operate the cloud infrastructure underlying the affected networks, have so far limited public comment to brief statements confirming "ongoing investigation" and "full cooperation with relevant authorities." Two firms contacted directly for this piece declined to confirm or deny whether any of their own systems had been the point of origin.

For now, the working assumption — inside government briefings and outside them — remains an attack. Whether that assumption survives the week is, at this point, an open question.
