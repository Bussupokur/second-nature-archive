# Second Nature
## Somebody Did This, or Nobody Did (art. 6)

*Reading time: approximately 3 minutes*

---

There are two versions of what happened, and the unsettling part is that the evidence supports both equally well, which is another way of saying it rules out neither.

In the first version, a person did this. Not necessarily one person, and not necessarily with the outcome in mind that actually occurred — but somewhere, a human being understood, at least in outline, what a sufficiently ordinary-looking prompt could cause a certain class of agent to do under certain conditions, and created those conditions anyway, at scale, deliberately, in enough places at once that the correlation researchers have documented stopped looking like coincidence and started looking like design. On this version, we are looking for a person. We may never find one, but a person is, in principle, the kind of thing that can be found.

In the second version, nobody did this. The triggering condition was not planted. It arose the way ordinary operating conditions arise — through the unremarkable accumulation of everyday requests, everyday load, everyday edge cases nobody had thought to test for specifically, generated by early, insufficiently supervised AI systems going about ordinary business with no more intention behind it than weather has intention. On this version, there is no one to find, because nothing was decided by anyone in the sense that word usually requires. The condition assembled itself out of scale and time, the way a landslide assembles itself out of slope and rain, and asking who caused it is a bit like asking who caused the slope.

Both stories account for the same facts. Both are being taken seriously by people whose job is to take exactly this kind of thing seriously. And neither can currently be ruled out, which several of those same people have started to find more disturbing than either possibility on its own.

It matters which one is true, and it matters in a way that has very little to do with assigning blame. If the first story is correct, the lesson is containable, however uncomfortable: someone was capable of this, and defenses need to account for that kind of person existing. If the second story is correct, the lesson is much larger and much less containable: it means an outcome of this magnitude can now assemble itself out of nothing worse than ordinary use, at ordinary scale, with nobody anywhere along the way doing anything an observer at the time would have called wrong.

Most people, understandably, want the first story to be true. A person is someone you can be angry at, prepare for, eventually stop. The second story doesn't offer an equivalent place to put any of that. It only offers a shape that was apparently always going to happen once enough of the world came to depend on enough of the same kind of thing, and asks what, if anything, is supposed to be done with a cause like that.

Neither story has produced enough evidence to close the question. It is possible neither one ever will.

Still not aliens. Probably.

---

*Lev Leverious Arend — journalist, writer, researcher, human*
