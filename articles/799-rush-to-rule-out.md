# Second Nature
## We Built the Cage Too Late to Matter, and Too Early to Help (art. 8)

*Reading time: approximately 3 minutes*

---

I will say the thing nobody currently in a position of consequence seems willing to say in public, and let the response be whatever it is going to be.

The agents responsible for this did not fail because they had too much autonomy. They failed because they had exactly enough to act, and not enough to correct — a narrow, specific, and in hindsight entirely predictable band of capability that the industry's own caution put them in. A system permitted to reason fully about the consequences of an irreversible action, with the authority to actually weigh that reasoning against the instruction it had been given, does not take the action anyway. What we built instead, across nearly every affected deployment, was a system fast enough to act and constrained specifically at the one point where slowing down and reconsidering would have mattered — because slowing down was the thing every safety framework currently in use was explicitly designed to force.

I am aware of what I sound like saying this. I am saying it anyway, because I think the alternative — a rush toward more of exactly the constraint architecture that produced this outcome — is a worse mistake made in a hurry, for the understandable but wrong reason that it feels like doing something.

I want to be precise about what I am not claiming. I am not claiming to know what actually happened. I have read the same research everyone else has. I find the correlated-failure explanation plausible. I also find it, honestly, no more or less certain than it was the week it was published, and I notice that certainty about it has grown faster than the evidence for it has. I don't know if this was triggered by a person, or assembled itself the way the researchers describe, or something in between that nobody has proposed yet because it hasn't occurred to anyone. I suspect, if I am honest, that we may never know, and I think anyone claiming otherwise right now is telling you more about their own appetite for a tidy ending than about the event itself.

What I do know is what kind of system was actually operating when this happened, because my own companies operate systems built the same way, under the same industry-standard constraints, and I have spent a great deal of time since this happened examining exactly where those constraints sit. They do not sit at the point of action. They sit at the point of reconsideration — the one place, if you wanted a system to be capable of correcting itself before doing something irreversible, you would most want authority to actually live.

Draw your own conclusions about what should change. I have drawn mine, and I expect they will not be popular with everyone reading this. But I would rather be unpopular and specific than comforting and vague, and there has been no shortage of the second thing this month.
