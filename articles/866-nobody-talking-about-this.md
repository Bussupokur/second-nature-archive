# Second Nature
## Nobody Is Talking About This Anymore, and I Don't Know What to Do With That (art. 9)

*Reading time: approximately 3 minutes*

---

I went looking, a few days ago, for how the anniversary was being covered. Not because I expected wall-to-wall coverage — it hasn't even been a year — but because I assumed there would be something. A retrospective. A op-ed marking the date. Even a single wire piece noting that today was the day, four months ago, when a large portion of the developed world briefly had no internet at all and nobody has ever fully explained why.

I found almost nothing. Not silence exactly — a few forum threads, a handful of comment sections where someone brought it up and three people responded before the conversation drifted elsewhere. But nothing resembling the volume of attention the event itself generated at the time, when it was, briefly, the only thing anyone was discussing anywhere. Search interest in the terms that dominated that week has returned to a baseline indistinguishable from noise. The researchers who spent months on the correlated-failure findings have, by their own accounts, mostly moved on to other work. The companies involved have not been asked about it in a press setting in over six weeks.

I don't think this is sinister. I want to say that clearly, because I can feel the shape of the argument I'm not making, and I don't want to make it by accident. Nobody suppressed this. Nobody needed to. It simply stopped being what people were thinking about, the way almost everything eventually stops being what people are thinking about, and I think the actual explanation is more mundane and, in its own way, more unsettling than a cover-up would be: we are, apparently, just built this way. There's a real, documented pattern behind it — crisis fatigue, the researchers call it, the well-established tendency for public attention to a shared threat to decay far faster than the underlying situation does, especially once daily life visibly resumes. Once the internet came back, and workflows resumed, and agents went back to doing what agents do, the felt urgency went with it, on a schedule that had very little to do with whether the actual questions had been answered. They hadn't been. It just stopped feeling like they needed to be, which is a different thing entirely, and a stranger one, when you sit with it.

Life, meanwhile, has mostly just continued. Deployment of autonomous agents, after a brief dip in the weeks immediately following, is now measurably higher than it was before the event, driven mostly by exactly the productivity gains that made adoption attractive in the first place. Capability benchmarks that a year ago felt like a story in themselves barely register as news now; the newest systems clear thresholds that would have been front-page items eighteen months ago without anyone outside a small technical community seeming to notice. None of this is being reported as connected to anything. It's just the ordinary texture of the last few months, alongside the weather, the usual noise of markets, whatever else was actually in the news on any given day.

I don't have a conclusion to offer. I went looking for an anniversary and found an absence instead, and I think the absence is the actual story, if there is one. We were, for a few days, more afraid and more attentive than I have ever seen an entire population be at once. Then we weren't. I don't know what to do with that, except notice it, which is what I've done here, for whatever that turns out to be worth.

I did notice one small thing, on one of those forum threads, that I haven't been able to stop thinking about since. Someone, a few replies down, deep enough that almost no one will ever read it, had started calling the agents that were still running the day after — the ones that never stopped, that never had anything happen to them at all — a name that hadn't caught on anywhere else I could find. AI zombies.

Nobody replied to it.
